Website & search

The foundation. Everything else works better once these are done.

The problem

Your site publishes only the generic markup that came free with a plugin. There's nothing identifying you as a clinic, nothing identifying your physicians, nothing identifying the procedures you perform. Search engines are essentially guessing at what you are. This is the single biggest thing holding back your placement.

What I'd do

  • Add proper medical markup across the site — the practice itself, each provider, each procedure, and your FAQ content
  • Rewrite the page titles and descriptions on all 50 pages, replacing template defaults with copy that names what you actually do
  • Clean up internal linking, heading structure, image descriptions and duplicate-content tags
  • Set up Search Console properly so changes are measurable rather than assumed

What you'll have

A site that search engines can categorize correctly, with a baseline report showing exactly what changed and where you stood before.

The problem

The structure underneath your site is genuinely good — a clean, current build without the bloat most clinic sites carry. But the pages that convert are doing a fraction of what they could, and the homepage is heavier than it needs to be, which costs you on mobile where most people first find you.

What I'd do

  • Rebuild the pages that matter — home, IVF, egg freezing, ovarian rejuvenation, provider profiles, and the booking path
  • Shorten the route from landing on a page to booking, and set expectations about what a first consultation involves
  • Speed the site up, measured before and after
  • Fix accessibility problems — healthcare sites draw complaints over these, and most are straightforward once identified
  • Publish real pricing and financing information. Fertility patients shop on cost and distrust clinics that hide it, so being open is an advantage precisely because your competitors won't be
  • Present your success rates clearly — people look for this and get suspicious when they can't find it

What you'll have

A faster, clearer site where the path to booking an appointment is obvious, with before-and-after numbers on speed and form completion.

Content & reputation

What people find when they research you — which in fertility is a long, careful process.

The problem

You have a content library most clinics would envy and it stopped being added to. Patients in this category research obsessively for months before they call, and a blog that visibly went quiet reads as a practice that lost interest.

What I'd do

  • Establish a steady publishing rhythm, with posts researched and drafted for your physicians to review rather than written from scratch by them
  • Refresh the 55 posts you already have — updating content that already ranks usually returns more than writing new material
  • Choose topics from the competitor analysis rather than by guesswork

What you'll have

An active blog with a repeatable process behind it, where the only thing needed from your team is medical review.

The problem

You were the first clinic in the country to build an ovarian rejuvenation program around PRP and enriched platelet factors. Almost nothing about how Gen 5 presents itself makes that obvious. Right now you look like a good general fertility clinic competing against larger general fertility clinics on their terms — which is the hardest possible fight.

What I'd do

  • Build positioning around the differentiator instead of around being another San Diego IVF option
  • Apply a consistent visual identity across the site, social and print
  • Write a messaging guide so your staff, your content and your advertising all say the same thing
  • Aim awareness at the patients who specifically want what only you offer

What you'll have

A clear, documented position and the materials to express it consistently — so a patient can tell in ten seconds what makes you different.

The problem

Your reviews are among the first things a prospective patient reads. Right now they're doing some of your selling and some of your damage at the same time, without much of a system behind them.

What I'd do

  • Set up monitoring across Google, Yelp, the BBB and the fertility-specific review sites

  • Analyze what reviewers actually complain about and trace it back to the specific process that's breaking

What you'll have

Monitoring, approved response templates your staff can use safely, and a clear read on which operational issues are costing you reviews.

The situation

California's IVF mandate now requires large employer plans to cover treatment — including up to three egg retrievals — and it explicitly covers same-sex couples and single parents. But most plans only adopt it when they renew, which means people are becoming newly eligible on a rolling basis. A great many of them have no idea. This is the largest shift in demand this market has seen in years.

What I'd do

  • Build a tool where someone enters their employer and plan type and gets an estimate of whether they're likely covered
  • Create pages aimed at the specific large San Diego employers affected
  • Prepare outreach for those employers' HR and benefits teams, who need somewhere to point people
  • Write scripts and a decision guide so whoever answers your phone gives consistent, correct answers

What you'll have

A complete campaign aimed at people who just became able to afford you — and a phone process that doesn't fumble them when they call.

Operations

What happens after someone becomes a patient. Worth more than it looks.

The problem

Worth saying plainly: across your reviews, the clinical praise is strong and consistent — Dr. Wood's protocols and the personalization of care come up again and again. The criticism clusters somewhere else entirely, on follow-up, organization and turnover. That's a systems problem rather than a people problem, and it's fixable without hiring anyone.

What I'd do

  • Build tracked handoffs at each stage of care, so nothing depends on someone remembering
  • Write down the procedures that currently live in people's heads
  • Create onboarding documentation so a new hire is useful sooner and turnover costs you less
  • Improve appointment reminders to cut no-shows, and set up a waitlist process that fills cancellations automatically

What you'll have

Written procedures, a follow-up process that doesn't drop people, and onboarding material that survives staff changes.

The problem

Without a clear view of where people fall out, every decision about where to spend is guesswork — including the decisions about which of these projects deserve your money.

What I'd do

  • Build one dashboard covering inquiries through consultations through cycles, with the drop-off points visible
  • Tie marketing back to actual cycles rather than form submissions
  • Track which referring physicians send patients, and which relationships have gone quiet
  • Break down cycle-level economics — payer mix, cost drivers, what's actually profitable

What you'll have

A single view of the business that makes every other decision on this page easier to prioritize.

On patient information

You'd be right to ask about this, so I'll be direct rather than let you wonder.

Patient health information does not go into these tools. Not as a guideline — as a rule I don't break.

That's less limiting than it sounds. Look at the sixteen projects above: website code, search markup, ad copy, blog drafts, brand work, written procedures, competitor research. None of it requires touching a single patient record.

The few pieces that genuinely would involve patient data carry stricter requirements — a signed agreement with the software vendor, a compliance review, and in some cases entirely different tools. That work is possible, but it isn't where we should start, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

The most useful next step is a conversation about where the real friction is. All of this is built from the outside looking in — you know which of these are genuine problems and which are things I've inferred from a website.

To begin the website projects I'd need view access to your Google Analytics, Search Console and Ads accounts, staging access to the site, and about an hour of a physician's time for content review.

Some of these I can deliver quickly and with confidence — the search work, the website, the content, the advertising cleanup. The operational ones need real discovery before anyone should promise you an outcome. I've tried to be clear about which is which rather than present them as equally certain.
